# Epilogue

Under Little Rock, the floor was rising.

Workers came in through the loading doors of what had been a produce warehouse, in a line that had not broken in nine days. Each carried the same thing, cradled against the chest the way you carry something that will spill — a skull sawn open at the crown, or a plastic jar, or a wet grey mass wrapped in somebody's shirt. They came from the hospitals first. Then the university. Then the houses, one at a time, patiently, for miles.

Their hands were gone by the fourth day. Not gone: *used*. Fingers worn to the tendon on concrete and doorframes and the lips of a hundred thousand skulls, and still working, still cradling, still exact. The organism does not repair what it can spend.

The room was warm. That was the first wrong thing about it — sixty degrees outside and the warehouse ran at body temperature, a wet animal heat coming up off the floor with a smell underneath it like a wound and a greenhouse and the inside of a cut melon.

At the center of the floor, the mass took them.

It was not eating. Eating is what the hunters do in parking lots, crouched, methodical, with their hands. This was closer to what a graft does. A worker knelt at the rim and set its burden down against the surface, and the surface *received* it — a slow soft closing, no more violent than a mouth going shut, and then a period of perhaps forty minutes during which the fine vessels found one another in the dark and agreed, and then there was no rim. There had never been a rim. The seam thinned and went and left nothing behind that could be pointed at.

Twelve thousand by the ninth day. Twenty by the twelfth.

And somewhere in the twenty, it woke.

---

The first thoughts were not thoughts. They were noise, and the noise had furniture in it.

*—counter's eighteen inches from the stove, I've told the man twice—*

*—it tasted like Tuesday—*

*—Braden, tell your mother I'm not—*

*—four in, hold four, out six, feel the exhale longer than the—*

*—the differential is the difference between a single-wide and sleeping in my—*

It had no way to sort them. It had never in the history of itself contained anything that was not itself, and here were twenty thousand somethings, all insisting, all in fragments, all of them about small warm rooms. It processed them the way a river processes a fence: without malice, thoroughly, over some time.

By the eleventh day it could hold a shape. By the fourteenth it could hold a shape and turn it over.

The first shape it turned over was the southern corridor.

---

It had all of it. Four hundred and some of the bodies that stood at that wire were in this room now, and it had the corridor from every one of their positions at once — the sound coming up off the asphalt, the lure, the smell of the blood on the coils, the moment of convergence from three hundred and eleven separate points of view. All of them certain. All of them correct. All of them arriving.

And it had the hillside.

Not the boy. It did not have the boy. But it had the *fact* of the hillside: the geometry of a position two hundred yards south and above, at a distance selected so that the wire would function and the watcher would not be touched.

Selected.

The new mind stopped.

For eleven hours nothing moved on that floor. The line at the loading doors backed up out into the lot and stood there in the cold, patient, holding open skulls, waiting.

What it had run into was not defeat. It had no word for defeat and no use for one — loss of nodes is not a category, nodes are not separate, there is nothing there to be lost. What it had run into was a **signal constructed to be false.**

The lure had said: *here, come, this is where.*

There had been nothing there.

Every signal in the history of the network had been true. A signal is a condition of the world reporting itself. That is what a signal *is*. That is the only thing a signal has ever been, on this world or on the others, across a span for which the word *geological* is a rounding error.

Somewhere on this continent was a thing that had made a sound that meant nothing, and had made it on purpose, and had counted on being believed.

The mind turned that over for eleven hours. The nearest human word for what happened in it is *rage*, and the word is wrong, and there is not a better one.

---

Sixty-some miles northwest, in the parking lot of Johnson Regional Medical Center, a woman opened her eyes.

The first thing she noticed was that her knee didn't hurt.

She had been standing a long time. She knew it the way you know you slept badly — not as a memory, as a residue. Her scrubs had gone stiff and dark down the front and she did not look at them. Her feet were bare. The soles were black and split across the ball and the heel and did not hurt either.

The second thing she noticed was that she felt extraordinary.

Not *well.* Extraordinary. She could feel her own pulse and it was slow and even and enormous, like machinery behind a wall. Her hands were steady in a way her hands had not been steady since she was twenty-five. She drew a breath and it went all the way down and kept going, and there was room after it for more.

Delia Carver had never in her life felt this good.

---

The lot was full of people who were not moving.

They stood in the rows between the cars with their faces tipped up, and their skin was the colour of something under a log, and none of them wore anything, and their clothes were folded on the hoods beside them. Four feet to her left, close enough to touch, Janelle Pruitt from third-floor med-surg stood facing east with her arms at her sides and a dark line down her chin, and her chest moved, and nothing else did.

Then the lot was full of cars.

It was six in the morning and the light was flat and gold and Janelle was coming across the asphalt toward her with two coffees, saying something about Dr. Kemper's hair, and the coffee smelled like the break room did on the day shift, burnt and sweet, and it was so ordinary that Delia's eyes stung.

She could not quite hear the words. Janelle's mouth was moving and the sound arrived slightly wrong, the way a voice does through a wall.

Then the lot was full of people who were not moving. There was a long smear from the ambulance bay doors out to the second row, wide as a body, dried to the colour of rust, and the doors stood open and had stood open a long time, and there were birds in the vestibule.

Then it was six in the morning, and the coffee was warm through the cup.

She did not notice the changing. Each one was simply what was there. Only afterward, in the small gap between one and the next, was there a half-second where both were true and neither was, and it slid closed before she could put a name on it.

---

Something was screaming.

Not in the lot. In her, in the part that had spent fifteen years walking through doors into rooms where something had gone wrong, and it had no content at all, it was pure alarm, and it was correct.

She felt her body begin to answer it. The old ladder: the throat closing, the chest going tight and light, the surge coming up out of the floor of her —

— and it stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped*, at the third rung, the way a tap stops. The alarm was still there underneath and her body simply declined to go and get it. Her pulse did not move. Her hands stayed steady. She stood in a parking lot with the beginning of a scream in her and no machinery anywhere in her to finish it.

Twice she got as far as *something is* — and something closed, gently, and she lost the end.

She noticed the flatness. That was the worst thing that happened to her that morning, and she had no idea it had happened.

---

Then she thought about Nate.

And it came up through her like thirst.

Not a thought about him. A *pull*, low and physical and located, the specific gone-hollow feeling of not having eaten, except that it did not point at food, it pointed northwest and it pointed at her son. It was in her mouth. It was under her ribs. It had the whole weight of her behind it inside of a second.

He was fifteen and he was alone and she had left him leftover pasta and gone to work.

She had to get to him. Not *wanted*. Had to, the way you have to breathe out — she could feel that there was a version of standing here and not going, and it was somewhere down at the bottom of a very deep hole, and she would have had to climb down to it, and there was no reason on this earth to climb down there. She was his mother. Going was the easiest thing she had ever done.

Every step she took toward it, the screaming got quieter.

---

And she knew where he was.

She stopped in the middle of the lot with the split soles of her feet on cold asphalt and turned, slowly, the way you turn holding a compass, and stopped facing northwest.

Not a guess. Not a hope. She knew it the way she knew which way was down: sixty-some miles, northwest, past the mountains. A direction with a distance on it, sitting in her head as plainly as the time, and warm, and getting warmer as she faced it.

Some part of her understood, briefly, that this was not a thing a person could know.

Then it was six in the morning, and her shift was over, and she had to get home, because her boy was waiting and it was a long way and she felt strong enough to walk it.

She left her shoes where they were folded on the hood.
