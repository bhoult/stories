## 0. Verdict

**The book is in much better shape than it was, and one continuity error is doing real damage.**

The Tier 1–5 work held. The calendar is coherent from Day 0 to Day 53. Journal numbering runs 1–21 plus 11b with no gaps. Em-dashes are down from 15.7 to **4.09 per 1,000 words** and the prose did not suffer for it. The justifying-clause decay curve is intact. Act Four grew from 7,816 to 14,200 words and now has a spine — Jim's death is the best chapter in the book and it earns the act title.

Against that: **§1.1 below invalidates the moral justification for the roadblock**, which is the novel's central event. Ruth's atenolol countdown is what makes the wolf knife necessary rather than optional, and Chapter 13 explicitly resupplies her with "months" of it twenty-two days before Nate refuses a half-day detour on the grounds that she has four days left. That is not a typo; it is the load-bearing beam under Act Three's climax, and it needs fixing before anything else on this list.

Everything else is ordinary second-pass work: nine mechanical errors, an unaudited epilogue with four internal contradictions, one structural redundancy worth ~2,000 words, and one genuinely large unclaimed opportunity that the new interstitial created and nobody picked up (§4.1).

## 1. Continuity — mechanical

### 1.1 Ruth's atenolol is resupplied for months, then runs out — and the roadblock depends on it ⚠️ **[MECHANICAL]**

**This is the most consequential error in the manuscript.**

| Where | Day | Text |
|---|---|---|
| Ch13:37 | 15 | Maria: *"Ruth has twenty-five of atenolol… We have time."* (pre-run) |
| Ch13:131 | 15 | The haul: *"four months of beta-blockers, three months of SSRIs…"* |
| Ch13:139 | 15 | *"Jim's metoprolol was replenished. **Months of supply. Ruth's atenolol, the same.**"* |
| Ch17:83 | 23 | *"Jim has **thirty days** of metoprolol… Ruth has **eighteen days** of atenolol."* |
| Ch21:85 | 37 | *"Ruth's atenolol supply was at **four days**."* |
| Ch21:128 | 37 | *"**Ruth has four days of atenolol**, and we can't carry the packs on an unpaved detour."* |
| Ch28:53 | ~42 | *"Ruth's atenolol runs out **tomorrow**."* |

Eight days after being given months of supply, she has eighteen days. Jim goes from "months" to thirty days in the same span.

**Why this one matters more than the others.** Ch21 line 128 is Nate's answer to Jim's *"Those are people."* The argument is: no time to detour, because Ruth's medication runs out in four days. That sentence is the hinge the entire roadblock swings on — it is what makes three hundred deaths a tragedy of constraint rather than a choice Nate made for other reasons. If a reader remembers Ch13, the constraint is fictional and Nate is simply impatient. Which, given §8.9's action-bias material and Nate's own confession that *"part of me wanted it,"* a hostile reader will happily conclude.

**Fix at Ch13, not at Ch17/21/28.** The scarcity chain downstream is consistent with itself (18 days at Day 23 → 4 days at Day 37 → out at Day 41–42 is exactly right) and it is doing necessary work. Ch13 is the outlier and it is one paragraph.

The cleanest version keeps the pharmacy's irony intact — the beta-blockers were on the shelf *because nobody knew to take them* — while constraining what a small-town independent actually stocks:

> Jim's metoprolol was replenished. The metoprolol shelf was full; it's the common one, and nobody had wanted it. Atenolol was three bottles. Ruth counted them twice and said "Well," which is what Ruth says instead of swearing.

That preserves the scene's point, gives Ruth a beat, and sets the countdown running. Then adjust the "four months of beta-blockers" summary line to attribute the bulk to metoprolol.

**Second-order check:** Jim's clock also needs to survive. Ch17 gives him thirty days at Day 23 → Day 53. He dies Day 50 with medication still available, which is correct and quietly important, because §1.2 of the death scene turns on whether the beta-blockade explains why he didn't turn. Don't let a fix to Ruth's supply accidentally run Jim out early.

## 2. The epilogue — first audit

1,793 words, written last, never reviewed. Four internal problems, all fixable in a sentence each. This section is longer than the others because the epilogue is the last thing a reader sees and the first thing a Book Two reader will re-read.

### 2.1 The mind wakes on day twelve and can hold a shape on day eleven **[MECHANICAL]**

> :15 — *"Twelve thousand by the ninth day. **Twenty by the twelfth.** And somewhere in the twenty, it woke."*
> :35 — *"**By the eleventh day** it could hold a shape. By the fourteenth it could hold a shape and turn it over."*

It wakes at day 12 and achieves coherence at day 11. Fix: *"By the fifteenth day it could hold a shape. By the eighteenth…"* — and note this also gives the eleven-hour stall at :51 more room, since the mind needs to have *had* the corridor memory long enough to turn it over.

### 2.2 Four hundred bodies, three hundred and eleven points of view, three hundred dead **[MECHANICAL]**

> :41 — *"**Four hundred and some** of the bodies that stood at that wire were in this room now, and it had the corridor from every one of their positions at once — the moment of convergence from **three hundred and eleven** separate points of view."*

Two figures in one sentence, and both exceed Ch21/Ch22, which put the toll at *"approximately three hundred"* and are explicit that Nate lost count. Ch22's Journal #15: *"I say 'approximately' because I lost count when the second wave came out of the woods."*

The fix is an opportunity, not a chore. Nate cannot count them. The organism can. Pick one exact number and use it once:

> *Three hundred and eleven of the bodies that stood at that wire were in this room now, and it had the corridor from every one of their positions at once.*

The reader who remembers *"approximately three hundred"* gets a small cold shock — the thing that ate them knows the figure Nate couldn't get. That is the epilogue's job in a single number.

### 2.3 Little Rock to Clarksville is a hundred miles, not sixty **[MECHANICAL]**

> :67 — *"**Sixty-some miles northwest**, in the parking lot of Johnson Regional Medical Center…"*

Johnson Regional is in Clarksville. Little Rock → Clarksville is ~100 miles. The manuscript's *own* "sixty-some miles northwest" is **Clarksville → Fayetteville**, used correctly nineteen lines later when Delia orients on Nate (:133). The same phrase is doing two different distances in one chapter.

Fix: *"A hundred miles northwest"* at :67. Keep :133 as is — the repetition of *northwest* across the two is good, the repetition of *sixty* is an error.

**Related, harder [JUDGMENT]:** the roadblock is on Highway 71 between Alma and Fayetteville, roughly 150 miles from Little Rock. The epilogue has workers carrying skulls on foot to the core mind over nine days. That is ~17 miles a day, barefoot, with hands worn to the tendon. It is not impossible and the passage's imagery of an unbroken line is strong enough that most readers will not run the numbers — but if you want cover, one clause does it: the line at the loading doors *"had not broken in nine days, and its far end was somewhere none of them could see."*

### 2.4 The memory fragments are Nate's and Jim's sentences **[JUDGMENT — but check intent]**

Four of the five noise fragments are verbatim or near-verbatim from characters who are **not** in the mass:

| Epilogue | Source |
|---|---|
| *"counter's eighteen inches from the stove"* | Ch1:38 — Nate, describing his own trailer |
| *"it tasted like Tuesday"* | Ch2:141 — Nate, eating his mother's pasta |
| *"four in, hold four, out six, feel the exhale longer than the—"* | Ch13 — **Jim's** breathing instruction; Jim is buried at Fayetteville |
| *"the differential is the difference between a single-wide and sleeping in my—"* | Ch1:18 — **Delia's** line, quoted by Nate; Delia is the Emissary, not in the mass |

If this is deliberate, it is the best idea in the epilogue and it is currently invisible — a reader will feel the déjà vu and assume authorial recycling rather than a signal. If it is accidental, it is a mechanism error, because the passage is explicit that the material came from Little Rock hospitals, the university, and local houses.

**Three ways to go, in order of ambition:**

1. **Make it deliberate and say so.** One sentence after the fragments: *"None of the twenty thousand had ever stood in that kitchen. It had the sentence anyway."* This turns a possible error into the epilogue's most frightening line — the organism's memory is not local, and something has been reaching further than the bodies it holds. It also arms Book Two: if the network already has Nate's interior monologue, the Emissary is not the first breach.
2. **Keep the echo, drop the impossible two.** Retain Nate's trailer details (a neighbour could plausibly have said the counter line; the differential line is Delia's, and Delia *is* connected). Cut or alter Jim's breathing count, which has no route into the mass at all.
3. **Replace all five with unfamiliar fragments.** Safest, and the weakest — the uncanny recognition is most of what the passage is doing.

I'd take (1). It costs one sentence and converts the largest ambiguity in the chapter into the hook.

### 2.5 Delia should have rooted six weeks ago **[JUDGMENT — mechanism]**

The book's rules, from Ch29 (Moss): rooting is a **maturity** progression, not a density one. *"The photosynthetic adaptation is stage one. Stage two is sessile conversion."* Ch28 finds ~90% of the Fayetteville population rooted by Day 42, and Nate's own tracking since Week One establishes that the **oldest** Turned green first, undress first, and root first.

Delia turned on **Day 0**. She is as old as any Turned in Arkansas. By the epilogue — after Day 53, and the core mind is at least fourteen days into its own reckoning — she should be grey-green, nude, feet in the soil, wired into a network.

Instead: standing, in scrubs, feet bare and split but free, skin unremarked. The chapter never addresses it.

The organism rebuilding her covers this completely — but the text has to *say* so, and right now the rebuild is described only as health (*"her knee didn't hurt"*, *"she felt extraordinary"*). Two clauses would close it and would make the rebuild more frightening rather than less:

> Eleven days ago they had brought her up out of the ground. The soil had closed over where her feet had been and there was nothing left in the grass to show it.

That also resolves the scrubs, which are currently the oddest detail in the chapter — everyone else in the lot is nude with their clothes folded on the hoods, and she alone is dressed. If the organism put her back in her scrubs, that is a choice, and the choice is *for Nate*. Which is the whole premise of Book Two, arriving one line early and for free.

### 2.6 "Twenty by the twelfth" reads as twenty, not twenty thousand **[MECHANICAL — cosmetic]**

*"Twelve thousand by the ninth day. Twenty by the twelfth."* The ellipsis is intentional and mostly works, but "somewhere in the twenty" three lines later compounds it. Consider *"Twenty thousand by the twelfth."*

Three chapters apart, with Jim's death between them. The death is supposed to reset everyone — and structurally it can't, because Ch34 has to re-establish positions Ch31 already established, which flattens the difference the death was meant to make.

**Ch34 is the better version.** It is a quarter the length, it is imagistic rather than sectioned, it observes from the roof rather than cataloguing, and it earns its place as the last chapter. Ch34's Caleb — counting breaths alone, on the step, at the time they used to do it, *"and Ruth has asked us all to leave him to it"* — is worth more than all of Ch31's Caleb section.

**What Ch31 has that is load-bearing and must survive:**

- **Jim's decline** (:11) — the setup for Ch32. Essential. Nate noticing the hand on the door frame and saying nothing is the exact failure he confesses in Ch32's opening.
- **Nate's sleep collapse** — *"Safety, it turned out, was when the bill came due."* This is one of the book's best ideas and it appears nowhere else.
- **The mirror moment** — his mother's jaw in his jaw, the machinery stopping. Essential.
- **The perimeter observation** — hunters testing the frequency edge, roots creeping into the bare zone. This is the plot's forward pressure and Ch34's version is scenery by comparison.

**What duplicates Ch34 and can go:** the Ruth, Dale-and-Maria, Marcus, Caleb, and Sienna sections as *sections*. Roughly **1,800–2,200 words**.

Recommended shape for Ch31: drop the bolded per-character headers entirely, open on Jim, run the sleep material, the mirror, and the perimeter. Let the others appear only where they intersect Nate — Marcus with the charged phone (which is genuinely his moment and should stay, but as a scene, not an entry), Sienna with the candy bars. The chapter becomes ~2,300 words, is *about* something rather than being a list, and Ch34's survey then lands as the first time we have seen the group whole since Jim died — which is what it is for.

## 4. Opportunities the current draft is leaving on the table

### 4.1 Delia calls Nate at 12:51 AM and he never sees the missed call ⚠️ **[MECHANICAL gap + the largest unclaimed moment in the book]**

The interstitial, :67–:85 — she gets into a supply closet, has two bars, calls him. It rings four times. She hangs up **before the tone** *"because a voicemail would frighten him."*

No voicemail. But a phone that rings four times leaves a **missed call**, with a timestamp.

Nate's phone is alive and in his hand constantly for the next thirty hours. Ch2 has him calling her at 9:42, again immediately, at 10:15, 10:30, 10:47, texting twice, calling the hospital main line, scrolling Reddit, reading news sites. Ch3 has him checking it at 14%, then using the last of the battery: *"I used the last of the battery to try Mom one more time. Voicemail. I didn't leave a message."*

He never sees that she called. He goes to his grave — and the reader goes to the epilogue — without either of them knowing she got to a closet, and thought about the pasta and the Sprite and the bag with the broken zipper, and decided against a voicemail because it would scare him.

**This is a continuity gap** (the missed call exists and the text ignores it) **and it is the single best unclaimed beat in the manuscript.** The interstitial was added precisely so the reader would hold something Nate doesn't. Right now the reader holds it forever. Giving it to him — once, late, at the right cost — is available and nothing else in the book can do this job.

Three options:

**(a) He sees it on Day 1 and misreads it.** In Ch2, among the calls: *`Missed call — Mom — 12:51 AM.`* He calls back. Voicemail. And because he is thirty-six hours into believing this is a medical emergency with a timeline, he files it as *she's busy, she'll call back*, and it becomes one more piece of evidence that she is fine. Then it detonates later, when he knows what 12:51 AM was. This is the strongest version — it costs three lines in Ch2 and it converts the interstitial from a nice piece of parallel construction into a loaded gun.

**(b) He never sees it and finds it later.** The phone is dead from Ch3 onward and stays in the trailer. There is no plausible route back to it — Ch18 explicitly declines to return for the textbook. This version requires machinery the book doesn't have.

**(c) He sees it and understands immediately.** Weakest. Spends the charge in the wrong chapter.

If you take (a), the payoff belongs in **Ch25**, on the center line, in the passage where he is doing the arithmetic on ninety-five miles. He has been carrying a missed call from 12:51 AM for thirty-nine days and has never known what it was, and the walk south is, among other things, a walk toward the only person who could tell him. That is a reason to go that the chapter currently has to argue for. It would also give Ch25 something it lacks: a fact, rather than a feeling, that pulls him.

**Cost:** three lines in Ch2, one paragraph in Ch25. **This is my strongest recommendation in this report after §1.1.**

### 4.2 Ch25 doesn't know about the vow Nate made in Ch22 **[JUDGMENT]**

Ch22's Journal #15 ends the roadblock chapter with a formal, witnessed-feeling decision:

> *"I am letting her go. Not the memory: the rage… I am putting it down the way I put down the hatchet: because my hand doesn't want to let go and I am making it let go."*

Three days later he takes the hatchet off his pack and walks south to kill her.

Ch25 never mentions this. It is a superb irony sitting one sentence away from being visible — the boy who writes everything down, who has just written down a renunciation, breaking it in seventy-two hours without noticing the contradiction until much later, if ever.

Ch25 already has the perfect location: *"I run everything. I had the numbers… And I started the sum somewhere around the first mile marker and then I put it down."* One clause connects them:

> I had written three days earlier that I was putting her down. I had written it in a numbered entry, which is the version I let people read.

That also strengthens §8.7's survivorship-bias material in Ch30 — the journal is not just incomplete about the world, it is unreliable about *him*.

### 4.3 Sienna has three lines of dialogue in Act Four **[JUDGMENT]**

Counting speech attributions across Ch29–34 (six chapters, ~14,300 words): **Ch29: 1. Ch30: 0. Ch31: 1. Ch32: 0. Ch33: 0. Ch34: 1.**

The book's second-most-important character, whose arc is *"I know where the edge is now"* and whose recovery is the counterweight to Nate's, spends the last sixth of the novel being observed through windows. Ch31's candy-bar scene is her only real presence, and it is good — *"Don't use your customer service voice"* is the last useful thing anyone says to Nate before the broadcast.

She is also absent from Jim's death, which is conspicuous. Ch32 accounts for everyone: Ruth has his hand, Caleb has two fingers, Dale cuts the cord, Marcus holds the door frame, Maria holds Dale, Moss and Howard come in. Sienna appears once, taking Caleb into the hall, and then is not mentioned again in a 4,028-word chapter.

Jim prays for her — *"He asked that she be given something to run toward"* — which is the single best line about her in the book and she is not in the room to hear it. That may be deliberate. If it is not, having her in the doorway when it is said, and having her leave, would cost four words and pay off in Ch34 when she is running.

### 5.2 "The machinery" is now a tic **[MECHANICAL count / JUDGMENT call]**

## 8. Closing

Two things are worth saying plainly.

**The first is about §1.1.** I have flagged it three times in this document because it is not a continuity error in the ordinary sense. Every other item on this list is something a reader might not notice. This one is load-bearing: it is the difference between Nate being trapped and Nate being impatient, and the book has spent §8.9 teaching the reader to watch for exactly that distinction — *"the difference between waiting to understand and waiting to avoid is that the first one has a date on it and the second one doesn't."* A reader who has absorbed that lesson and then catches the atenolol contradiction will conclude the roadblock had no date on it either. Fix Ch13 and the accusation dissolves.

**The second is about §4.1.** The interstitial was added to give the reader something Nate doesn't have. It succeeded, and in succeeding it created an object — a missed call at 12:51 AM — that exists in the world of the book and is currently unclaimed by it. That call is the only remaining route by which Nate can learn that his mother got somewhere safe, thought about him, and chose not to frighten him. The book's entire emotional argument is that he never gets to know what happened to her. Letting him hold the fact and not the meaning, for thirty-nine days, and then handing him the meaning on the center line of a dead highway, is the version of that argument with the most force. It costs a few lines.

Everything else here is maintenance. The book is close.
