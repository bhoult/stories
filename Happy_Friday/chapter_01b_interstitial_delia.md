# Interstitial: Delia's Shift

She packs the lunch she won't eat.

Turkey on wheat, an apple, a Diet Coke that will still be cold at four in the morning because the break room fridge runs too hard and nobody has ever called anyone about it. She puts it in the insulated bag with the broken zipper. She has been meaning to replace the bag for two years.

The boy is on the couch. He has been on the couch since seven this morning, when he told her his stomach hurt in a voice she recognized from her own childhood, and she put her hand on his forehead and felt nothing and let him have it anyway, because it is October and he is fifteen and the fight would have cost more than the day was worth. The Sprite and the sleeve of Saltines are still on the counter where she lined them up. He has not touched either one. He has eaten most of a bag of Doritos.

She does not say anything about the Doritos.

"I'm on at six," she says. "There's leftover pasta in the fridge. Don't stay up all night."

"I won't."

They both know this is a lie. It is a small one. She has a list of things worth spending a Friday on and this is not on it.

She checks her phone before she puts it in her scrub pocket. Nothing from Kevin. There has been nothing from Kevin for a long time now, in a way that stopped being an event and became a fact, the way weather becomes a fact. Her sister has texted about Thanksgiving. She will answer at some point tonight, in the hour between two and three when the waiting room empties out and the world holds still.

At the door she turns back and points at him. It is not a gesture that means anything. It is the gesture she makes.

"Happy Friday, bud."

"Happy Friday," he says, and does not look up.

She leaves at five forty-five because she is always early.

---

The drive is fifteen minutes of dark. She listens to half of a podcast about a woman who disappeared in Missouri in 1994. She parks in the third row because the second row floods when it rains and it has been raining all week.

The lot is normal. The doors are normal. The sliding doors open the way they have opened ten thousand times and she goes in past the sign about visiting hours and the sign about masks that nobody has updated since whenever, and she clocks in at 5:52.

Friday nights have a shape. Nothing happens until nine. Between nine and midnight it is the drunk-and-disorderly hours, the closing-time fractures, the man who put his hand through a window and cannot explain why. After two it is the chest pains, the old people whose bodies choose the middle of the night to make an announcement. She has been doing this for fifteen years and four of them here, and the shape of a Friday is as familiar to her as the shape of her own kitchen.

She checks the board. Six patients, none of them interesting. Janelle tells her that Dr. Kemper has cut his hair and that it is a serious problem, and they look at Dr. Kemper across the department for slightly too long, and Janelle says *see*, and Delia says *I see it*, and that is the whole conversation and it takes ninety seconds and it is the last ordinary thing.

She eats half the turkey sandwich at eleven, standing in the break room, reading her sister's text about whether anyone still likes the sweet potato thing with the marshmallows on it. She types *nate does* and then deletes it and types *i do* and sends that instead, because it is true and because it is easier.

The first ambulance comes in at eleven fourteen.

---

The man on the gurney is grinning.

The paramedic bringing him in is a kid named Ruiz who has worked this route for three years and has seen what there is to see, and Ruiz's hands are not steady on the rail. He says the call came in as a possible stroke. He says the guy was standing in his driveway. He says the guy has not stopped doing that since they picked him up, and he tips his chin at the man's face in a way that Delia will think about later, in the small amount of later she gets.

The grin is too wide. It uses muscles that a smile does not use. The eyes above it are not participating.

Delia has seen facial droop and she has seen seizure and she has seen the particular blankness of a man whose brain has been interrupted, and this is none of those. She reaches for a penlight because that is what you do, and somewhere underneath the part of her that is reaching for a penlight, something older is standing up very slowly and paying attention.

"Sir, can you tell me your name?"

"It's a beautiful morning," the man says, pleasantly.

It is eleven fourteen at night.

---

By midnight there are four more. By twelve-thirty the triage line is out the doors and the doors keep opening for it. Two of the new ones are grinning and three of them are not, and the three who are not are the ones screaming, and Delia understands with a clarity that arrives all at once and without any evidence at all that the screaming ones are the ones in trouble.

She does not know why she believes this. She will not get time to find out.

The department goes wrong the way a wave goes over a wall — not all at once, but in one direction only, and faster than anyone standing there can revise their plan. Someone pulls a fire alarm. Someone else silences it. A woman in a Razorbacks sweatshirt stands up in the waiting room and starts to howl and does not stop, and two of the grinning ones turn their heads toward her together, and Delia is running before she has decided to.

She is a nurse. The room is full of frightened people. Somebody has to not be.

At twelve fifty-one she gets to a supply closet on the north corridor and pulls the door shut and takes out her phone. Two bars. She calls him.

It rings.

She thinks, very clearly and in words, about the pasta in the refrigerator, and whether he will eat it, and about the fact that the Sprite is still sitting on the counter where she put it. She thinks about the bag with the broken zipper. She thinks that she should have said something else at the door — not something better, just something *more*, some second sentence to sit beside the first one, because *Happy Friday, bud* is not very much to have said to somebody.

It rings.

Her heart is going hard. She knows exactly how hard. She has counted ten thousand pulses with two fingers on other people's wrists and she does not need to count her own to know that it is high and climbing, and that she has been awake for seventeen hours, and that the sertraline she has taken every morning for six years is doing something for her right now, though she has no way of knowing what and no reason to think about it.

It rings.

Outside the closet door, in the corridor, someone says her name in a perfectly friendly voice.

It rings a fourth time.

*You've reached Delia Carver.* The stumble on the last name, still there, four years on. *Leave a message and I'll call you back.*

She hangs up before the tone, because a voicemail would frighten him, and she has one more thing to do out there, and the department is full of people who are afraid.

She puts the phone back in her pocket. She puts her hand on the door.
