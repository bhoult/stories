# *Happy Friday* — Editorial Report

**Manuscript reviewed:** `Happy_Friday.md` (30 chapters, 120,923 words) plus `outline.md` and `characters.md`.
**Scope:** whole-manuscript continuity, plot logic, factual accuracy, character consistency, redundancy, structure, and prose. Full read-through, no sampling. No text has been edited.

---

## 0. Verdict First

This is a good book with a fixable problem set.

The premise is genuinely fresh — an apocalypse where the transmission vector is your own adrenaline, which inverts every survival-fiction convention and makes the *least* capable person in the room the most survivable. The execution honors that premise with unusual discipline: guns make things worse, fortification is suicide, the fat kid who can't run is right, and the recovering addict's twelve years of craving management turn out to be the most valuable skill set on Earth. Four or five sequences (the Knock, the septic tank, Jim punching Sienna, the roadblock, the Civilization VI monologue) are as good as anything in the genre.

The problems are almost entirely artifacts of how it was written — chapter by chapter, without the whole text in view. They cluster into five groups:

1. **A calendar that drifts.** Day counts, months, elapsed-time phrases, and medication clocks disagree with each other in roughly forty places.
2. **One excised chapter that left a hole.** The outline's Chapter 23, "Nate Walks," was replaced by "Sienna Stops." Three separate passages in Chapters 22, 23, and 24 still reference the deleted scene. This is the manuscript's only real *plot hole*, and it is a bad one — Chapter 22 ends on a cliffhanger that is never resolved.
3. **Four planned third-person interstitials that were never written.** Their absence is the single largest craft cost in the book.
4. **The organism's behavioral taxonomy is explained to the reader six times.** This is the largest available word-count cut.
5. **Act Four is 6% of the book** and functions as an epilogue rather than an act.

None of this requires re-conceiving anything. Estimated work: a hard continuity pass, one restored chapter, two or three new interstitials, and a compression pass targeting ~13,500 words.

## 1. Timeline and Calendar

### 1.1 The anchor

Chapter 3 fixes the outbreak: *"Friday, October 24th."* Chapter 5 confirms *"Day three. Monday."* Day 0 = Friday, Oct 24, and every subsequent Day N = October 24 + N.

But Chapter 27 shows Moss's printout reading **Comet C/2026 Voss**, and the outline confirms 2026 is intentional (series canon). **October 24, 2026 is a Saturday.** October 24 falls on a Friday only in 2025, 2031, 2036.

**Fix (one-word):** change the date to **October 23rd**, which *is* a Friday in 2026. This preserves the comet designation, the September tail-passage, the "six weeks before the outbreak" discovery window, and the Monday = Day 3 anchor. Do not change the comet — the outline's series arc depends on it.

## 2. Plot Holes and Dangling Threads

### 2.1 THE BIG ONE: the missing Chapter 23 — **RESOLVED**

The outline's **Chapter 23, "Nate Walks,"** was a third-person chapter from Sienna's POV: Nate wakes at 3 AM two nights after seeing his mother and walks back toward the hospital; Sienna follows; he breaks down on the road; **she puts her hand over his mouth and says "Quiet. Quiet. I know. But quiet."**; Marcus finds them and **physically carries Nate back**.

That chapter is not in the manuscript. In its place is a different scene ("Sienna Stops"). Three orphaned references remain:

1. **Chapter 22 ends** on: *"I took the binoculars and walked. Back up the highway. Back the way we'd come. Back toward Clarksville."* **This is never resolved.** Chapter 24 opens with Nate asleep in camp. A reader will assume they skipped a page.
2. **Chapter 24** has Nate recall *"the way she'd put her hand over his mouth on the highway and held him and said 'quiet, quiet, I know.'"* **No such scene exists in the book.**
3. **Chapter 25** opens: *"I'd been there for two days and I don't remember much of it except Sienna's hand and Jim's shoulder and **Marcus carrying me**."* **Marcus never carries him on the page.**

All three trace to the same deleted chapter. This is the manuscript's one true plot hole and it sits at the emotional climax.

**Recommendation: restore the chapter, but reframe it.** The outline's version — Nate sobbing "ugly and loud and completely uncontrolled," Sienna clamping a hand over his mouth, Marcus physically carrying him back — should not be restored as written, for two reasons:

1. **The book already spent that card.** Chapter 21 has Nate collapse on the hillside after the roadblock, make "sounds like a wounded thing," and run into the woods. A second uncontrolled breakdown two chapters later is a repeat, and the weaker of the two.
2. **It breaks the book's own physics.** Sustained loud sobbing in the open at 3 AM is exactly the catecholamine-plus-noise profile that turns people or summons hunters. Sienna's hand over his mouth is the outline patching that hole, and the patch is the part that reads as melodrama.

**Reframe as surrender, not collapse.** Nate's failure mode has never been screaming — it's *stopping*. The manuscript establishes this repeatedly: Ch22's "I stopped on the highway… there was nowhere to move to"; Ch25's "you stop being the person who processes and become the person who is processed"; Ch5's hypothermic drift where "my rationality was failing, and the failure felt like comfort." Act One is literally titled **INERTIA**, and Nate's original sin is sitting at a window while Lisa Anderson is taken. A quiet give-up chapter makes his lowest moment a *relapse into who he was*, which no version of a sobbing fit can do.

The reframe also inverts the threat in a way nothing else in the book does. Giving up is not a spike — it's a drop. He goes *below* threshold. The Turned ignore him completely. He can sit down on a highway at 3 AM in November and be perfectly safe and quietly die of it, and **nothing comes for him.** That is a fresher horror than a chase, and it lets him walk toward the organism's "warmth / invitation" — seeded in the septic tank (Ch14) and in Sienna's near-conversion (Ch15) — voluntarily, because it is offering exactly what he now wants: permission to stop being himself.

**RETRACTED — the "restless / searching" Turned (Ch5).** Nate flags a sub-mode he can't explain and marks it with a question mark; hunting mode is named eight chapters later. **RETRACTED as a defect — Jim's declining heart.** Deliberately left open for Book One. The question of whether Act Four should collect this cost is a structural choice, and it lives in §6.1 where it belongs — not here as an oversight.

**RETRACTED — the compound bow (Ch20).** Nate takes it because everything he knows about survival says a weapon is a thing you should have. It never becomes useful, and that is the point.

## 6. Structure

### 6.1 Act balance

| Act | Chapters | Words | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| One: Inertia | 1–7 | 18,863 | 15.6% |
| Two: Congregation | 8–17 | 47,628 | 39.4% |
| Three: The Walk | 18–26 | 46,677 | 38.6% |
| **Four: Inheritance** | **27–30** | **7,755** | **6.4%** |

Act Four is an epilogue wearing an act's clothes. It has real material in it — Nate's nightmares arriving *because* he's finally safe is the best psychological writing in the book, and Moss's "the failures are the curriculum" scene earns the whole novel's thesis — but four chapters averaging 1,900 words cannot carry a title like "INHERITANCE" or an epigraph like *"I survived. I wish that felt like enough."*

**Options:**
- **(a)** Grow Act Four to ~15,000 words by collecting a cost. Jim's decline is already loaded and cocked; the act title is literally about what gets passed down. Jim dying — or visibly beginning to — and handing something to Nate would make the epigraph true.
- **(b)** Retitle and re-epigraph Act Four to be honest about what it is (a coda), and move the act break earlier so Act Four begins at Chapter 26 (arrival).

I'd take (a).

### 6.2 The four planned interstitials were never written

The outline specifies a formal architecture: *"Intercut with Nate's chapters are third-person interstitial sections that show events Nate can't witness: **Delia's last shift at the hospital, the Lyles' final hours from inside their house, Marcus's three days alone in his truck cab, Dr. Moss's early research as the university empties around her.**"*

**None of them exist.** Chapter 24 carries the label but is third-person-limited *on Nate*, not on another character — so the book never once leaves Nate's knowledge.

This is the largest craft cost in the manuscript, and it's most visible in two places:

**"Delia's Shift."** The outline is right about why it matters: *"This interstitial exists so that 'Happy Friday, bud' belongs to Delia before it belongs to the Turned."* As written, the reader meets Delia for four paragraphs in Chapter 1 and never sees her again except as a corpse-in-motion. Every later payoff — the Knock in Ch4, the Whataburger tell, the hospital parking lot in Ch18, Journal #16's single devastating line — is asking the reader to grieve a character they barely met. **The single highest-leverage addition to this manuscript is writing that interstitial.** It's ~1,200 words and it would raise the ceiling on eight later scenes.

**"The Lyles' Final Hours."** Chapter 16 is powerful, but it is entirely observed through binoculars from three blocks away. Nate says so himself: *"I don't know what happened inside the house. I will never know the full sequence — whether Craig broke first or Brett."* That's a good line for Nate and a bad deal for the reader. The outline's plan — cut inside the house for the argument, the mother's voice, the decision to open the door — would convert the book's second-biggest set piece from a spectacle into a tragedy.

**Marcus's three days in the cab** and **Moss alone on an emptying campus** are lower priority but would both do work: Marcus's arc depends on a despair the reader is told about rather than shown, and Moss arrives at 85% of the way through the book with no interiority at all.

The tonal contrast is also a stated design goal (*"the third-person interstitial chapters should be stark, stripped of Nate's wry distance"*) that the current manuscript can't achieve, because 121,000 consecutive words of one voice is a lot of one voice.

### 6.3 Chapter lengths are wildly uneven

Range: **682 words** (Ch7) to **10,914** (Ch21) — a 16× spread.

- **Ch7 "The Pattern" (682 words)** is not a chapter. It's the closing beat of Ch6. Merge it.
- **Ch21 "The Wolf Knife" (10,914 words)** is three chapters: the roadblock operation, the aftermath walk and Nate's collapse, and the gas station + chickens. The chicken/towel scene — one of the best comic beats in the book — is currently buried 9,000 words into a massacre chapter, where its tonal whiplash works against it. **Split into three.** This also fixes §2.1: a "Nate Walks" chapter restored after Ch22 would sit naturally in a re-chaptered Act Three.
- **Ch4 (1,258), Ch22 (1,395), Ch24 (1,562), Ch30 (991), Ch31 (984)** are all sub-1,600. Short chapters are fine as a deliberate rhythm — Ch22 and Ch30 in particular earn their brevity — but right now the variance looks accidental rather than designed.

### 6.4 Two "other survivors" encounters do the same job

The outline's **Chapter 20, "The Other Group"** — Phil's Walmart, where a soft-spoken former store manager sedates newcomers against their will because *"if fear kills, eliminate the capacity for fear"* — was cut. Its replacement in the manuscript is the Highway 71 gas station: nine people behind barbed wire who won't leave.

That's a straight downgrade. The gas station is **the Lyles again** — same lesson (fortification kills), same outcome (they won't listen), same Nate reaction. Phil was the only encounter in the plan that attacked *Nate's own doctrine*: he starts from the same true premise and follows it to a monstrous conclusion, which is the one argument the book never has to answer. Sienna's outline line — *"They're not survivors. They're the Turned with better management"* — is the sharpest thing in either document.

**Recommendation:** restore Phil, or repurpose the gas station into Phil. It costs nothing structurally (the gas-station chapter already exists in the right place) and it converts a redundant scene into the book's best thematic test. Note that the outline's Lesson Placement Guide has Phil carrying the prisoner's-dilemma payoff, which the manuscript currently sets up in Ch5 and then never lands.

### 6.5 The outline's own directive on lesson placement is partly violated

> *"Conceptual lessons (cognitive dissonance, neural plasticity, game theory, prisoner's dilemma) should be spread throughout the narrative, arising when Nate naturally notices their application — **not front-loaded as textbook reading sessions.**"*

The manuscript has five stop-the-story teaching passages: the *Brunner & Suddarth* reading (Ch4), the prisoner's dilemma (Ch5), the Red Queen hypothesis (Ch11), HALT (Ch20), and radical acceptance (Ch25). The first three are integrated well enough. **HALT and radical acceptance are the weakest — they're the same lesson delivered twice, four chapters apart, both times as Dale lecturing a seated audience.** Merge them into one scene and let the rest of Dale's philosophy emerge through his hands, which is what the carvings are for and what they already do beautifully.

Also missing: **neural plasticity** is in the Lesson Placement Guide as a named through-line and never appears in the manuscript at all.

## 7. Redundancy — Where the Word Count Is

### 7.1 The behavioral taxonomy is explained six times

Herding / swarming / hunting / rooting — plus the density threshold and the fear mechanism — get a full or near-full re-presentation in:

1. **Ch8**, Journal #3 — "Three behavioral modes. Three different signals."
2. **Ch11** — Moss's 12-minute broadcast, *then* Nate's "layered model" recap at the chapter's end, *then* Journal #6 recapping the recap. **Three passes in one chapter.**
3. **Ch13/16** — journal entries restating swarm and hunting mechanics.
4. **Ch17** — the committee meeting presents all of it again in four numbered sections (3,671 words, most of it recap).
5. **Ch27** — Moss presents all of it *again* on a whiteboard (2,524 words).
6. **Ch28/29** — Nate presents all of it *again* in the broadcast.

By Chapter 27 the reader has been told the taxonomy so many times that Moss's whiteboard — which should be the moment the machinery snaps into focus — reads as review. Worse, it *demotes Nate*: he spent 100,000 words earning this knowledge and then sits in a lecture hall being told it.

**Fix:** make Ch27 about what's *new* — the extraterrestrial biochemistry, the chlorophyll analogs, the roots-as-dendrites, the wired-vs-wireless emitter problem, terraforming, and the termite-mound rebuttal (that speech is excellent and should stay). Cut every sentence where Moss tells Nate something he told the reader. Nate's whiteboard correction (*"'Uncontrolled destruction' is an understatement"*) already shows you the right move — do more of that and less of the lecture.

**Estimated savings: 4,000–6,000 words.**

### 7.2 Chapter 17 is a recap chapter

The committee meeting's four numbered sections are structured as a presentation *to the group* of things the reader watched happen in Chapters 11–16. The chapter's genuinely new material is: Maria's pivot from "can we kill them" to "can we *use* them," the four protocols, Marcus's line to Jim (*"Don't forget the living so you can keep vigil over people who are already gone"*), and Jim's capitulation. That's maybe 1,800 words of content in a 3,671-word chapter.

**Two narrow trims, both in Act Two:**

- **Ch8 duplicates Ch13's formula.** *"because I notice things, it's what I do instead of being useful"* (Ch8) and *"It's the thing I do instead of being useful"* (Ch13) are near-identical five chapters apart. Ch13's is the keeper — it's the one the prose immediately dissects. Vary or cut Ch8's.

But it's not enough."* Ch16: *"Timing things is what I do when I can't do anything else"* plus *"I watched through binoculars… and did nothing."* One per chapter is sufficient; the doubling is what makes Act Two read denser than it is.

**Estimated savings: negligible (under 100 words).** This is a tuning note, not a cut.

**Guardrail for new material:** anything written for Act Three or Four should not reintroduce the reflexive version. If Nate diminishes himself past the roadblock, it must be an *earned conclusion* about his judgment, not a *habit* about his worth — the two read completely differently at that point in the arc.

### 7.5 Sienna delivers substantially the same speech twice

**Ch14** (steeple, after the septic tank):
> *"You keep doing this thing… The* I'm just a fat kid in a steeple *routine… you keep **building this case** — this really smart, really funny, really airtight case — for why you can't be responsible for anything… you use the smart to **build reasons why you can't act, instead of plans for how to act better**."*

**Ch18** (church parking lot, four chapters later):

Near-verbatim, including the case/plans antithesis. Both scenes are strong; together they blunt each other, and the Ch18 narration then adds *"She didn't know I had already given myself this talk,"* which flattens it further.

**Fix — differentiate rather than delete.** Ch14 is better placed (the failure is concrete and hours old, and it ends on *"the next time you go alone and leave a note, I might not be standing at the door when you come back"*). Give Ch14 the **diagnosis**. Then let Ch18 be **escalation, not repetition**: she already said this, he didn't change, and now three people are dead at the Lyles'. Ch18's genuinely new and sharper idea is *"in the best case scenario, everything that goes wrong is your fault — because if it's your fault, that means you had the power to prevent it."* Build Ch18 around that alone, strip the case/plans language, and cut *"She didn't know I had already given myself this talk."*

## 8. Prose and Voice

### 8.1 What the outline asked for vs. what's on the page

The outline's voice note is specific and good:

> *"Nate's intelligence shows through specificity and pattern-recognition, not through sustained Latinate abstraction. He might use 'catecholamine' once because he read it somewhere, then call it 'stress juice' forever after… He doesn't sound like an essayist — he sounds like a kid who absorbed a lot of adult language without fully metabolizing it."*

The manuscript is closer to the essayist. Nate is frequently articulate in a way that's beyond fifteen not in *vocabulary* but in **syntactic control** — long, balanced, professionally cadenced periodic sentences with parallel clauses. It's very good prose. It is not quite a fifteen-year-old's prose.

The places where the voice *is* perfectly calibrated are the ones the outline predicted: the Civ VI monologue in Ch26, the diazepam-vs-towel chicken calculation in Ch21, the spatula in Ch6, *"I am alive because an alien organism used the wrong woman's memory of a drive-through"* in Ch4. **These are the best-voiced passages in the book and they all work the same way** — technical precision applied to something absurd, then deflated. There should be more of them and fewer paragraphs of measured abstraction.

### 8.2 Measurable tics

Across the full manuscript:

- **2,036 em-dashes** — one every 59 words. Roughly a third are doing work commas or full stops could do better.
- **59 instances of "the way you…" / "the way a…"** as a simile frame. It's a signature and it's a good one; at 59 it's a mannerism.
- **15 instances of "It wasn't X. It was Y."** The outline explicitly flags this: *"Be cautious with 'This is not X, it is Y' phrasing."*
- Most chapters end on a short aphoristic paragraph or one-line drop. Individually effective; thirty in a row becomes a metronome. Vary the cadence — end three or four chapters mid-motion instead.

Trimming these is where the 3,000 words of line-level compression in §7.7 comes from, and it will make the remaining instances land harder.

### 8.2b The justifying clause — the tic should decay and doesn't

Nate rarely performs an action without explaining to the reader why he is the sort of person who performs it: *"I put my thumb against my wrist and counted, because counting is what I do."* Broader than the self-deprecation in §7.4b — this is the habit of **justifying himself to the reader**, and it includes the meta-frames (*"I want to be clear about this because…," "I want you to know about these hours because…," "I am putting this in the journal because…," "I should record that"*).

**Useful early, irritating late.** In Act One it's the narrative contract, and Ch1 declares it outright: *"I'm going to be explaining a lot of things in detail going forward and you should probably get used to it."* A frightened fifteen-year-old writing a survival document for strangers over-explains. That's characterization. But a narrator who is still asking permission to tell you things at Day 45 has not grown, and the justifying clause quietly undercuts authority — a confident narrator simply tells you.

**Current density (per 10,000 words):**

| Act | Now | Should be |
|---|---|---|
| One (Ch1–7) | **3.2** | 3.2 — correct, leave alone |
| Two (Ch8–17) | 1.7 | ~1.7 |
| Three (Ch18–27) | 1.9 | **~1.1** |
| Four (Ch28–30) | 2.6 | ~1.3 |

It plateaus instead of decaying, and ticks *up* in Act Four.

**Cut list — four instances, all in the back half:**

| Ch | Text | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 19 | *"I am putting this in the journal because it should contain the truth: I was sitting on a rock…and I was happy."* | Preamble guarding an admission. The sentence is stronger starting at *"I was sitting on a rock."* |
| 20 | *"I want that recorded because the journal has been a catalog of horrors and the forest deserves better."* | Precious. The description that follows makes the case by itself. |
| 21 | *"I broke. **I should record that.** The analytical machinery failed…"* | *"I broke"* is stronger alone; the permission-slip weakens it at the book's most naked moment. |
| 25 | *"I want you to know about them because the version of this story that only contains fear and strategy and running is not the true version."* | **Duplicates Ch8** — *"I want you to know about these hours because they matter as much as the crises."* Same frame, same argument, sixteen chapters apart. Keep Ch8's; it's the original and it's better placed. |

**Do not cut** — these look like the pattern and aren't:

- **Ch19:** *"That's how I'm going to describe it because describing it accurately would require writing words I'm not ready to see in my own handwriting."* The *because* clause is the content — an act of concealment, not a throat-clear. See §7.3.
- **Ch21:** *"I'll have a plan because that's what I do now."* The confident inversion. See §7.4b.
- **Ch31:** *"counting is how she loves us."* Frame turned outward onto Maria.

**Result:** Act Three drops to ~1.1 and Act Four's two survivors are both inversions rather than justifications — producing the decay curve the voice needs.

**Guardrail for new material:** this tic is the most visible signature in Nate's voice, which makes it the easiest thing to over-apply when writing new chapters in his register. The first draft of the restored Ch24 ran at **44 per 10k — twelve times the manuscript's worst chapter** — before correction. Worse, it was the wrong chapter for it: Nate's machinery is *off* in that scene, and a narrator busy framing and justifying is a narrator whose machinery is running. **Prose should get flatter as he goes under, not more self-conscious.** Check any new Nate material against the table above before adding it.

### 8.3 The withheld-scene move in Ch21 — works as written

*Earlier drafts of this report flagged this passage as a craft problem. That was a misreading; the note is retracted and recorded here so the reasoning is on file.*

*"That is all I will ever say about this. What happened is not for a journal or a story to be told, there was no lesson to learn. Those hours belong to nobody, I wish I could forget them myself."*

What is withheld is the detail of Nate's own emotional behavior across those hours; the implication is embarrassment, not event. Read that way, the passage is doing three things correctly:

- ***"I don't know what happened in those hours"* is evasion, not a memory claim.** It is immediately followed by *"I know… I know… I know…"* — he substitutes what he's willing to say for what he isn't. The narrator has an established pattern of precisely this move (*"math is where I hide when the thing I'm saying is too ugly to say straight,"* Ch12; *"Not humility. Armor,"* Ch13).
- **The preceding paragraphs don't foreclose the gap — they describe Sienna.** She sat, she didn't touch him, she didn't speak; plus the environment and the endpoints. What *Nate* did in between is the blank. The account is closed around the wrong person on purpose.
- ***"There was no lesson to learn"* is load-bearing.** The journal's whole premise is that every entry extracts a lesson from a failure — Moss's *"the failures are the curriculum."* An entry that refuses because there is nothing to extract is that framework's single exception, and the strongest line in the passage.

**One small note in service of the intent, not against it:** *"there was no lesson to learn"* is currently buried mid-sentence behind a comma splice. Repairing the splice promotes it to its own beat, which is where it belongs.

**Mechanical only:** comma splices in both final sentences; double space after *"about this."*

## 9. What's Working — Don't Touch

Listed because a report this long can make a book sound worse than it is:

- **The premise and its inversions.** Guns are worse than useless. Fortification is the killer. Courage is punished. The depressive, the addict, and the medicated seventy-two-year-old are the most survivable people in the county. This is a genuinely new idea, executed with discipline.
- **The Knock (Ch4).** The Chick-fil-A vs. Whataburger tell is the best single piece of plotting in the book — a life saved by an organism grabbing the wrong woman's memory of a drive-through.
- **The septic tank (Ch14).** Disgust as the one reflex older than fear. Horrifying, funny, and mechanically sound.
- **Jim punching Sienna (Ch15).** *"I hit you because I didn't know another way to stop it and I am sorry and I would do it again." / "Good."* Perfect.
- **Jim generally.** Holding metoprolol and the metaphysics of grace in one hand without collapsing either is the best-drawn character in the book, and *"Those aren't mutually exclusive"* is its thesis in three words.
- **The pharmacy inventory (Ch13).** Narcotics gone, beta-blockers untouched. *"That is a metaphor for the entirety of the human condition."* Ruth is right.
- **The roar as an evolved anti-fortification siege weapon (Ch16).** The best conceptual payoff — it retroactively explains the military checkpoints, the Lyles, and every fortified position in the book.
- **The rooting reveal (Ch26).** Everything seeded from Chapter 2 — the sun-facing, the greening, the undressing — converges. Structurally the most satisfying moment in the manuscript.
- **The Civ VI monologue (Ch26)** and **Ruth's towel (Ch21).** The two funniest beats, both perfectly placed against horror.
- **The cantaloupe (Ch19).** *"I had earned the tongue to taste it."*
- **The wooden chickens (Ch18).** Dale carving what Caleb can't keep. The book's best small gesture.
- **Journal #16.** One line. Leave it exactly as it is.
- **Dale's ruined and finished foxes carried in opposite pockets.** *"What the hands could do, and what the shaking had done, and both of them his."*
- **Marcus's "You can't save people who don't want to be saved. That's not a failure. That's a boundary."**
- **Nate's nightmares getting worse once he's safe (Ch29).** The most psychologically true thing in the book.

## 11. One Closing Note on the Ending

The last line — *"Then I went downstairs to help Marcus with the truck. I didn't know anything about trucks. I'd learn."* — is the right ending.
